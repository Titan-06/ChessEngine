# Empirical Analysis of Chess Search Algorithms in Menso

Menso is a chess engine I wrote in C ([repository](https://github.com/Titan-06/ChessEngine)). This doc goes through four search techniques I added to it, how each one changed the numbers on six standard positions, and how the final engine does in actual games.

**A longer and more casual article on the analysis can be found [here](https://titan-06.github.io/blog/analysis-chess-engine/). This analysis is a condensed formal version of that original write-up and it's done so using AI. The measurements and interpretations are based on my experiments and remain the same for both, still I have reviewed this analysis against the original write-up.**

For every technique I look at why I added it, what it did to the metrics, and my guesses about why the numbers came out the way they did. Where a guess is still untested, I say so.

## 1. Scope and conventions

**Board representation.** I went with [0x88](https://www.chessprogramming.org/0x88). Bitboards were out because they were too hard to wrap my head around in the time I had, and 0x88 gets off-board checks done quickly with binary operations.

**Move legality.** Legal moves are generated by making each pseudo-move and checking whether the mover's king ends up attacked. Checkmate and stalemate are the same check ("no legal moves left"), the only difference being whether the side to move is in check. I tested the special moves (en passant, castling, promotion) with [perft](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Perft).

**What the engine doesn't have.**

- Threefold repetition. The engine has no idea what repetition is, so it walks into draws even from winning positions.
- The fifty-move rule and insufficient material. [cutechess-cli](https://github.com/cutechess/cutechess) handles these, but since the engine doesn't know about them, it can't avoid them.
- Iterative deepening and time management. Every game is played at a fixed depth with an infinite time control.

**Terminology.** When I say "move" or "depth", I mean one ply (one player's move).

**Evaluation.** I kept it simple on purpose: material plus piece-square tables. More heuristics means more work per node, which means less depth in the same time, and an engine with tons of knowledge at low depth usually loses to a material-only engine that searches deeper. And no matter how good the evaluation is, the engine will never see a 7-8 move mate coming if it doesn't have the depth.

| Piece | Weight |
|:---:|:---:|
| Pawn | 10 |
| Knight | 30 |
| Bishop | 30 |
| Rook | 50 |
| Queen | 90 |
| King | 999 |

The final score is `5 * square_score + 100 * piece_score`, from the side to move's point of view. The piece-square tables are from the [Chess Programming Wiki](https://www.chessprogramming.org/). I use one table per piece for the whole game, which isn't ideal, since what's good positionally in the middlegame can be bad in the endgame.

## 3. Search algorithm results

### 3.1 v1: Negamax (baseline)

[Negamax](https://chessprogramming.org/Negamax) is a depth-first search of the whole move tree. It behaves the same as its sibling [minimax](https://chessprogramming.org/Minimax); the only difference is how you think about it, and you only need one function. It evaluates every single node to the target depth, so this is the baseline for everything else.

**What stands out**

- Position 3 has the lowest EBF, which makes sense since it's an endgame with fewer legal moves.
- Positions 2, 4, 5 and 6 have a higher NPS than positions 1 and 3. I believe that's because they branch more, so a bigger share of the nodes are leaves. Leaf nodes don't have to generate legal moves, which is the slow part of internal nodes, so they're cheaper and push NPS up. This matters again when we look at the later versions.

The problem with negamax is that it looks at everything, even moves that are clearly great or clearly terrible. That's where alpha-beta pruning comes in.

**What stands out**

- Position 2 used to be the slowest, but now it's position 6, which has the highest node count and EBF. My guess is the symmetry and the lack of special moves. Pruning struggles when no moves stand out. And it depends heavily on move order: a great move searched first cuts everything after it cheaply, but the same move searched last means evaluating every branch before it. The move order right now is more or less random, so the engine needs to look at the aggressive moves first, like captures and en passant.

### 3.3 v3: MVV-LVA move ordering

[MVV-LVA](https://www.chessprogramming.org/MVV-LVA) (Most Valuable Victim, Least Valuable Attacker) sorts the generated moves so captures come first. Victims are ranked queen, rook, knight, bishop, pawn, and attackers from cheapest to most expensive (pawn, bishop, knight, rook, queen, king). So the engine looks for the most material it can win using the piece it minds losing the least. It doesn't guarantee the first move is the best, but it raises the chance of finding a good move early. Quiet moves aren't sorted, and there are plenty of other ordering techniques that I didn't get to.

**What stands out**

- Node count and NPS both went down compared to v2, with some positions changing way more than others.
- The results are pretty inconsistent. MVV-LVA worked really well in some positions (2 and 5, over 80% fewer nodes than v2) and not much in others. That makes sense, since it's only one piece of the move-ordering puzzle. Sorting captures by material is good, but not enough on its own.
- Position 6 still has the highest EBF, though it does get a decent node reduction.
- Position 1 has the smallest reduction, which is expected: it's the starting position, so there are no captures to put first.

So the search to a given depth is now much cheaper. But the engine is still prone to dumb mistakes, specifically ones involving a long capturing sequence. It might be winning material after a series of captures, but if the search stops before the sequence ends, it can think it's actually losing. That's the [horizon effect](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Horizon_Effect).

> "The Horizon Effect is caused by the depth limitation of the search algorithm, and became manifest when some negative event is inevitable but postponable. Because only a partial game tree has been analyzed, it will appear to the system that the event can be avoided when in fact this is not the case." ([Chess Programming Wiki](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Horizon_Effect))

Is the fix more heuristic knowledge in the evaluation? Maybe, but running a pile of heuristics on every node just to sort out some capture sequences at the end of the search isn't worth it. A cheaper idea: when the main search ends on a capture, keep looking ahead through the captures until there aren't any left.

### 3.4 v4: Quiescence search

[Quiescence search](https://www.chessprogramming.org/Quiescence_Search) keeps searching capture moves after the fixed-depth search ends, until no captures are left. This one is different from the previous three: they were about cutting node count and time, but quiescence search makes both go up A LOT, in exchange for better moves.

**Move explosion.** In its basic form it blew up so badly in position 2 that I couldn't get any data even at depth 3. With no depth limit, it keeps going until there are no captures left, and in capture-heavy positions that takes a VERY long time. So I made three changes to speed it up:

1. **Delta pruning.** Similar to alpha-beta, it stops quiescence search when we're already so far behind that no capture is likely to save us. The 9000 comes from the queen's weight, and 500 is a margin to make it a bit more than that.

   ```c
   // Delta pruning
   int big_delta = 9500; // queen weight plus margin: don't look further when already down a lot of points
   if (static_eval < alpha - big_delta) {
       return alpha;
   }
   ```

2. **A depth limit of 2** for quiescence search after the alpha-beta search. This does weaken it, since it's no longer searching until the position is actually quiet.

3. **Capture-only move generation.** I was using the same function to generate every legal move, but quiescence search only needs captures. So the function now takes an extra parameter to get either all moves or captures only.

One thing to note: MVV-LVA lives inside the legal-move generator, so skipping the quiet moves also cuts the work of copying them in after the sorted captures.

All of these together made the search faster, since quiescence nodes are cheaper to process than regular alpha-beta nodes, so NPS went up. But I added them all at once, so I couldn't test how much each one contributed. My guess is that the depth limit of 2 helped the most, but that needs more testing.

## 5. Limitations

- **Fixed depth, infinite time.** No iterative deepening or time management, so all strength tests are at fixed depth 5. Elo is really a measure of how an engine plays in a tournament setting, so these results aren't a true Elo measurement.
- **Single timing run.** Each position was timed once with `clock()` on a 2-core laptop CPU. I haven't measured how much the timing and NPS numbers move from run to run.
- **Unexplained NPS behaviour.** The big NPS gap between depth 4 and depth 5 shows up in every version and I don't have a confirmed explanation.
- **Untested hypotheses.** My explanations for the NPS changes (leaf vs internal node costs) are reasoning, not measurements. I never counted leaf and internal nodes separately.
- **Quiescence changes not isolated.** Delta pruning, the depth-2 limit and capture-only generation went in together, so I don't know what each one did on its own.
- **No repetition handling.** The engine draws a lot of positions that might have been wins, which pulls down its score in every match.
- **Sample sizes.** 100 games per Stockfish level and 100 games for v4 vs v3, so the error margins are wide (e.g. +/- 46.5 Elo in the v4 vs v3 match).
- **Simple evaluation.** One piece-square table per piece, with no middlegame/endgame distinction.
- **Stockfish as a reference.** The limited Stockfish levels are an approximate reference, and their spacing in these results is uneven.

## 6. Future work

If I ever get back to this, these are the next steps:

1. **Threefold repetition**, so it stops throwing away winning positions.
2. **Iterative deepening and time management**, so it can be tested at real time controls and not only at fixed depth.
3. **Better move ordering**, like killer moves and the history heuristic.
4. **An endgame king table**, since the current one only suits the middlegame.
5. **Measuring each quiescence change on its own**, and counting leaf and internal nodes to check my NPS explanations.
